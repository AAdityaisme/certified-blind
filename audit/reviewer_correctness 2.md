## The Three Most Dangerous Findings

### 1. C3 (6.7× cost blowup) does not survive proper scaling

The flagship deployment finding is an artifact of StandardScaler assigning z-score 109.66 to `has_code_fence=1`, because that feature fires on only **2 out of 24,051 training prompts** (std = 0.009). With **RobustScaler**, the fraction of prompts routed to the strong model under code-fence shift drops from **1.000 to 0.206** — the "routes everything to GPT-4" behavior disappears. With no scaler, it drops to 0.653. This is confirmed by the E3 verification: `shift_c / always_strong_c = 1.0000` exactly — the shifted cost equals GPT-4 cost to 6 decimal places, which is impossible unless every single test prompt is routed to GPT-4, which only happens because one binary feature with z=110 dominates the linear decision boundary.

A reviewer will write: *"The 6.7× cost blowup is not a property of surface routing; it is a property of fitting a logistic regression on a near-constant binary feature and then extrapolating that feature to 1.0 for every test sample."*

**Fix:** Replace StandardScaler with RobustScaler or clip features at training quantiles. Or replace has_code_fence (binary, rate=0.008%) with a proportion feature (code_fence_frac = count of ``` per character). Rerun E3. The cost ratio under RobustScaler is estimated at ~1.2–1.5× (from frac_strong=0.206) — still notable but far less dramatic. The honest C3 becomes: "surface_logreg trained without code-fence examples is brittle when deployed against code-fenced inputs — but this is a train/test distribution mismatch, not a fundamental property of surface routing."

---

### 2. The eval-identity confound explains claimed C1 AUC

The core C1 claim — "surface-only models match intent-aware semantic models at AUC ≈ 0.71" — is explained by both model types learning **eval-name identity** from surface features, not task difficulty.

Measured evidence:
- Surface features predict eval_name with **69.6% top-1 accuracy** (86 classes, chance = 1.2%)
- AUC from **eval_name-only base rate** = **0.700** — matching or exceeding the best reported model
- Per-eval base rate std = 0.291; range [0.000, 1.000] — extreme eval-to-eval variation
- Under eval-name **GroupShuffleSplit**: tfidf drops 0.710 → **0.573**; semantic drops 0.696 → **0.622**
- Under **leave-one-eval-out** (5 largest evals): all models collapse to **0.477–0.593**, near chance

The conclusion that "the benchmark is surface-saturated and structurally cannot reward intent" is backwards. The correct conclusion is: **the benchmark has extreme per-eval base rate variation, and surface features identify benchmark identity well enough to exploit that variation.** This is a property of the train/test contamination from shared eval membership, not a deep property of LLM task difficulty.

This is actually a stronger argument for the paper's meta-thesis (benchmarks are gameable by form-reading), but it requires a complete rewrite of C1's framing and evidence.

---

### 3. Continuous scores silently thresholded without disclosure

All 11 models in RouterBench have **non-binary scores in {0.0, 0.1, 0.2, ..., 0.9, 1.0}** — not just 0 or 1. Over 7,700 score values per model fall in the range [0.1, 0.9], and **13,418 cells have score exactly 0.5**. The paper uses `solve_threshold=0.5` without disclosing this, so scores of 0.1–0.4 (a model that partially answers) are labeled "not solved" identically to 0.0 (complete failure). Scores of 0.5 sit exactly on the boundary.

This matters for label validity: the pairwise label `route_premium = 1 iff weak model fails` assumes a binary notion of "fails." When weak scores 0.4 and strong scores 0.6, is routing to strong genuinely necessary? This boundary case directly affects which prompts constitute "routing opportunities" and which the paper claims surface models mis-handle.

The paper's description ("route_premium = 1 iff the weak model fails") presents the label as binary-clear. Reviewers who examine RouterBench will find it is not. A threshold sweep (0.5, 0.75, 1.0) is required in the appendix.

## Recommended Fixes (Ordered by Impact)

1. **E3 rerun with RobustScaler** (or clipped StandardScaler, or feature reparameterization). Report the corrected cost ratio; scope the claim to "train/test format mismatch with near-constant features."
2. **Add grouped-split and LOEO results** to E1 as the primary evaluation. Reframe C1: under eval-held-out evaluation, all models are near-chance, confirming that no model generalizes routing intent across benchmarks.
3. **Threshold sweep** (0.5, 0.75, 1.0) in appendix; disclose that RouterBench scores are not binary.
4. **Disclose SIV mechanism** for code_fence: note that has_code_fence fires on 0.008% of training data, making OOD extrapolation the dominant mechanism. Add retraining experiment with 10% code-fence augmentation.
5. **Verify tfidf SIV=0**: test whether TfidfVectorizer's internal tokenization normalizes doubled whitespace (making whitespace perturbation a no-op for tfidf). Disclose if so.
6. **Report IS per-eval and cross-eval** alongside overall IS. Cross-eval pairs are necessary to support the cross-benchmark intent-sensitivity claim.
